\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

One of the central goals of computational genomics is to predict
biological function directly from DNA sequences. This goal encompasses
tasks ranging from identifying functional elements, such as
promoters, enhancers, transcription factor binding sites, and splice
sites; to chromatin accessibility, gene expression, or estimating the
functional effect of genetic variants.

To address this diversity of problems, DNA foundational models (FMs)
have been developed in recent years. These models are pretrained using
self-supervised objectives on large collections of genomes to learn
generalizable features, which can then be adapted to different tasks
through fine-tuning.

Transformer-based and SSM-inspired architectures have become the dominant approach in DNA modeling and FMs. Recently, the use of CNNs, which was the dominant approach before transformers, has also been re-explored in ConvNova \citep{boRevisitingConvolutionArchitecture2025}. See section \ref{sec:related_work_transformers_and_ssm} for more details.

DNA consists of two strands, where each one is the reverse complement(RC) of the other. Because a double-stranded sequence and its reverse complement describe the same physical molecule, a model that can't recognize this equivalence is wasting capacity relearning it separately for both orientations. We refer to this equivalence, that S and RC(S) encode the same physical molecule, as DNA's RC-symmetry.

One of the main challenges of DNA modeling is designing the model architecture to exploit the RC-symmetry of DNA. It has been shown that making the model treat the RC on it's input in an invariant or equivariant way improves performance as seen in \citet{zhouBetterUnderstandingReverseComplement2021} or Caduceus \citep{schiffCaduceusBiDirectionalEquivariant2024}. See section \ref{sec:related_work_zhou} for more details.

Formally, if \(S\) is a DNA sequence, \(f(S)=y\) is the ground-truth
function, and \(RC(S)\) is the reverse-complement of \(S\):
\begin{itemize}
	\item An RC-invariant task is one where \(f(RC(S))=f(S)\), such as ChIP-seq results, for example (since they work on double-stranded DNA)
	\item An RC-equivariant task is one where \(f(RC(S))=T(f(S))\), with \(T\) being
	a known transformation over the output space
	\item A non-canonicalizable task is one where $f(S)\neq f(RC(S))$, and there isn't a known $T$ transformation. For example, in a promoter classification task, the RC of a sequence labelled as
	non-promoter might or might not correspond to a promoter. Therefore, the
	transformation of the negative class under RC cannot be determined
	solely from its original label.
\end{itemize}

While \(RC(S)\) is a deterministic and reversible transformation of
\(S\) --- knowing \(S\) implies knowing \(RC(S)\) --- this does not
imply that these strands are functionally equivalent. \citet{moeckelStrandAsymmetriesGenomic2023} show that functional asymmetries are produced by several biological
processes. Several of
these asymmetries are associated with transcription, of which a few examples are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item A GC-skew between the template and coding strands
	\citep{greenTranscriptionassociatedMutationalAsymmetry2003}, which
	favours the production of structures such as R-loops and G-quadruplexes
	(\citet{ginnoRloopFormationDistinctive2012},
	\citet{maoDNAGquadruplexStructures2018}, reviewed from \citet{moeckelStrandAsymmetriesGenomic2023})
	\item Repetitive elements show significant transcriptional asymmetry
	\citep{georgakopoulos-soaresAsymmetronToolkitIdentification2021}.
	\item Certain elements, such as promoters, are only functional when correctly
	oriented \citep{nagawaRelationshipTATASequence1985}.
\end{itemize}

It's hypothesized that even though making the model equivariant or invariant to the RC is empirically beneficial(as shown in \citet{zhouBetterUnderstandingReverseComplement2021}), leaving room for the model to be partially asymmetrical and allowing it to model those asymmetries shown previously may improve performance.

Based on the available literature, the only model that explicitly
incorporates this asymmetry is CrossDNA
\citep{luoExplicitDynamicCrossStrand2026}, focused on modeling
physical interactions of double-stranded DNA. However, we did not find other works
that incorporate it to condition the processing orientation in sequence
prediction tasks. Most models focus on:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*]
	\item Processing both DNA strands in
	a fully symmetric way, as in the RCPS, Conjoined models
	\citep{zhouBetterUnderstandingReverseComplement2021} and Caduceus
	\citep{schiffCaduceusBiDirectionalEquivariant2024}
	\item Not explicitly incorporating this symmetry into the model architecture and instead letting the model learn it independently, as in Nucleotide Transformer
	\citep{dalla-torreNucleotideTransformerBuilding2025}, HyenaDNA
	\citep{nguyenHyenaDNALongRangeGenomic2023}, Zhou's RC-aug models
	\citep{zhouBetterUnderstandingReverseComplement2021}, and ConvNova
	\citep{boRevisitingConvolutionArchitecture2025}
\end{enumerate}

Although models that implicitly capture this symmetry could
theoretically learn it, and even be capable of accounting for the
asymmetries observed in \citet{moeckelStrandAsymmetriesGenomic2023}, better results are observed when
it is incorporated explicitly into the architecture, as can be seen in
the decreased performance of \citet{zhouBetterUnderstandingReverseComplement2021} RC-aug models or the general
improvement of Caduceus compared to HyenaDNA
\citep{schiffCaduceusBiDirectionalEquivariant2024}.

However, the best-performing model does not fit into either of the two
previous categories. ConvNova outperforms Caduceus, the second-best
model, by approximately 10 points despite having significantly fewer
parameters \citep{boRevisitingConvolutionArchitecture2025}, despite not
imposing RC invariance or equivariance in its architecture: it is
symmetry-aware (it takes both strands as input) but lets the network
modulate the symmetry instead of forcing it through weight-tying. This
suggests that neither ignoring symmetry nor fully forcing it is optimal,
and provides grounds for exploring an intermediate direction: being
symmetry-aware without forcing invariance or equivariance.

Another way to model this "semi-symmetry" consists of using a model
that processes both DNA strands and that, in order to specifically
address transcriptional asymmetry, always receives them in a consistent
orientation: the template strand and the coding strand occupying
predefined positions within the input.

Passing both strands in this orientation-consistent processing would
allow the model to capture the asymmetries that occur only in that
specific strand, since if it were simply given both strands in either of
the 2 orientations, the model would be forced to learn the biological
signals that occur equally on both strands (and become RC-invariant with
respect to transcription)

The orientation of the strands received by the model could be made
consistent through a canonicalization function. \cite{kabaEquivarianceLearnedCanonicalization2023}.~successfully
uses canonicalization for a wide range of domains, and proposes that
this canonicalization function should be learned rather than hand-engineered

Based on this, we present a proof of concept of this canonicalization, in which:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Genome annotation data is obtained to determine which features are transcribed, and which are the template/coding strands
	\item Using ConvNova, chosen for being symmetry-aware without imposing RC invariance/equivariance (see Methods), this method is evaluated on the Nucleotide Transformer benchmark \citep{dalla-torreNucleotideTransformerBuilding2025}, Genomic Benchmark \citep{fengBenchmarkingDNAFoundation2025}, BEND Benchmark \citep{marinBENDBenchmarkingDNA2023}, and on chromatin profile prediction using the DeepSEA dataset \citep{zhouPredictingEffectsNoncoding2015}. It is shown to produce an improvement over \ldots{}
	\item The future path towards a learned canonicalization function that does not depend on data external to the sequence is discussed.
\end{enumerate}

This canonicalization method could theoretically be applied to any architecture (it just wouldn't make sense in the case of an RC-equivariant or RC-invariant architecture).

Since the GFF constitutes the most reliable source available for
inferring transcriptional orientation, these results should be
interpreted as an upper bound on what is achievable for transcriptional
asymmetry through a learned function. 
\section{Related work} 

\subsection{Transformers, CNNs and SSM-inspired architectures}
\label{sec:related_work_transformers_and_ssm}

CNNs, such as Basset \citep{kelleyBassetLearningRegulatory2016}, Basenji \citep{kelleySequentialRegulatoryActivity2018}, and LegNet
\citep{penzarLegNetBestinclassDeep2023} were the dominant approach in DNA modeling before the rise of transformer-based architectures.

In recent years, transformer-based architectures such as
Nucleotide Transformer
\citep{dalla-torreNucleotideTransformerBuilding2025}, Enformer
\citep{avsecEffectiveGeneExpression2021}, DNABERT-2
\citep{zhouDNABERT2EfficientFoundation2024} have become the
dominant approach as FMs thanks to attention's ability to focus on
specific signals.

Recently, architectures such as HyenaDNA
\citep{nguyenHyenaDNALongRangeGenomic2023} and Caduceus
\citep{schiffCaduceusBiDirectionalEquivariant2024} have become popular because of their ability to capture long range dependencies without the large computational cost of transformer-based architectures.

\subsection{RC-aug, RCPS and Conjoined models}
\label{sec:related_work_zhou}
In \citet{zhouBetterUnderstandingReverseComplement2021}, 3 types of CNNs are evaluated on predicting 919 chromatin features(TF binding, histone marks and DNase I hypersensitivity). Each of the models take a single DNA sequence as input, represented as a matrix where the row is the nucleotide and the column is the nucleotide position. The models evaluated were:
\begin{itemize}
	\item RC-augmented/RC-aug models: The data was
	augmented by taking the RC-aug of the sequence and keeping the target
	value, and the model was trained normally
	\item RCPS (Reverse Complement
	Parameter Sharing) models: The network's filters are designed so that
	\(f(S)=f(RC(S))\)
	\item Conjoined (CJ) and Conjoined post-hoc models: The
	outputs of \(S\) and \(RC(S)\) were computed using the same network and
	then the outputs of both results were averaged.
\end{itemize}

The models were evaluated on multiple DNA datasets, where it can be
observed that the RCPS and Conjoined models were consistently the best.

\subsection{Canonicalization}

Canonicalization offers a third strategy for handling symmetries,
distinct from both data augmentation and architectural weight-tying.
Instead of forcing invariance within the network (as in RCPS) or
implicitly teaching it through the data (as in RC-aug), canonicalization
transforms the input to a standard orientation before it enters the
model, so that the network never needs to explicitly learn or impose the
symmetry.

Before \cite{kabaEquivarianceLearnedCanonicalization2023}, other frameworks were proposed in specific and
non-generalized forms, such as spatial transformer networks
\citep{jaderbergSpatialTransformerNetworks2015}. Kaba et al.~formalizes
and generalizes this approach, making the model \(\phi(x)\) be: \[
\phi(x)=h'(x)\cdot f\left( h^{-1}(x)\cdot x \right) 
\] Where:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \(h(x)\) predicts the transformation that the canonical
	version of \(x\) underwent, and its inverse is taken to bring \(x\) to
	its canonical version
	\item \(f(x)\) is the prediction function 
	\item \(h'(x)\)
	predicts the transformation that the \(y\) of that canonical \(x\)
	underwent. On RC-invariant tasks, $h'(x)$ is the identity function for all $x$
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{kaba.png}
	\caption{A diagram of the canonicalization process for a task where the goal was to recognize MNIST digits rotation in an RC-equivariant way, were the digit is transformed to it's canonical form, recognized, and then the classification is rotated back to it's original form. Figure adapted from \cite{kabaEquivarianceLearnedCanonicalization2023}}
\end{figure}

It is important to note that this work uses only the general concept of
canonicalization: transforming the input to a standard orientation
before processing it. It does not adopt the specific techniques that \citet{kabaEquivarianceLearnedCanonicalization2023} proposes to make this function learnable end-to-end.

Based on the available literature, there are no prior works that apply
canonicalization, learned or hand-engineered, to DNA sequence models
using external biological annotation as the source of the
transformation.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Network architecture}
ConvNova was used for the evaluation of the canonicalization's performance, which is a CNN consisting of a series of Gated
Convolution Blocks (GCBs), which consist of a gated convolution
\citep{yuFreeFormImageInpainting2019} with dilated filters
\citep{yuMultiScaleContextAggregation2016}, with an MLP or pooling at
the end depending on the task.

The model takes the DNA and its reverse-complement as inputs
\(\text{A}\) and \(\text{B}\) and computes the output as follows, making
the features of \(\text{A}\) be updated depending on the gating imposed
by \(\text{B}\)
\begin{gather*}
h=\text{GELU}(\text{convolution}(\text{A}))\\
g=\text{sigmoid}(\text{convolution}(\text{B}))\\
\text{A}=\text{A}+h\odot g\\
\text{B}=\text{B}+g
\end{gather*}

\begin{figure}[h!]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[height=0.3\textheight]{convnova_arch.png}
	\caption{Diagram of ConvNova's architecture. Figure adapted from \cite{boRevisitingConvolutionArchitecture2025}}
\end{figure}

The dilation rate of the GCBs increases as \([1,1,d,d^2,d^3,\dots]\) for
each block. At the end of the series of GCBs, the output \(A\) is passed
through an MLP before being passed to the downstream head.

ConvNova was chosen because it achieves state of the art metrics with a
relatively small number of parameters, because using an RC-invariant or
RC-equivariant architecture would nullify the effect of
canonicalization, and because by taking both DNA strands, it could more
clearly see the asymmetries between the forward and RC strands mentioned
earlier.

\subsection{Canonicalization}

The canonicalization algorithm consists of a pipeline that consists of:
1. Extract all transcribed features (including pseudogenes), their
positions, and whether they are oriented in coding or template form in
the dataset. The orientation of the features is obtained directly from
the `Strand' field.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\setcounter{enumi}{1}
\item
  To classify features as transcribed or not: For annotations without a
  standardized type field (NCBI), the Sequence Ontology hierarchy
  \citep{eilbeckSequenceOntologyTool2005} was used to programmatically
  determine which features correspond to transcribed units; for GENCODE
  annotations, the `Feature' and `Transcript type' fields were used
  directly.
\item
  Based on that, the goal is for all features to be in the coding
  orientation (chosen arbitrarily) by taking the reverse-complement of
  their coordinates in the genome.
\item
  In the case where there are overlaps between features of opposite
  orientation, applying RC to one segment is considered to
  \textbf{completely} destroy the other, since one of the features would
  be partially canonicalized. There are 2 possible options for how to
  resolve that window of overlapping features:

  \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*.]
  	\tightlist
  	\item
  	Bring all features from - orientation to + orientation, destroying
  	the nucleotides of the features in the + orientation
  	\item
  	Bring all features from + orientation to - orientation, and then
  	take the RC of the entire window of overlaps to bring everything to
  	the + orientation, destroying the nucleotides of the features in the
  	- orientation
  \end{enumerate}

  Of these 2 options, the option that destroys the fewest total
  nucleotides is chosen (in the case where they both destroy the same amount,
  option 1 is chosen arbitrarily). To obtain the coordinates of the
  overlap window, the overlaps of each segment are explored recursively.
\end{enumerate}

Multiple canonicalization algorithms were evaluated. For details on the different algorithms, see section \ref{sec:methods_optimal_canonicalization_search} for more details on the different algorithms evaluate and section \ref{sec:evaluation_canonicalizaiton_algorithms} for the evaluation protocol.

For the downstream tasks, their DNA sequences are aligned, using
minimap2 (\citep{liMinimap2PairwiseAlignment2018}) in the case where the
coordinates are not given and only the sequence is available along with
the genome from which they were taken. We refer to this genome as the
source genome, and this alignment is performed to determine whether the
sequences fall on a transcribed feature and must be canonicalized.

If more than 50\% of the sequence must be transformed, then the entire
sequence is transformed along with its label. The label transformation
depends on the task, and they are divided into 3 categories: -
RC-invariant tasks where the target value of \(S\) is the same as that
of \(RC(S)\) - RC-equivariant tasks where the target values are not
equal, but the target value of \(RC(S)\) can be known from that of \(S\)
- Tasks where the label of \(RC(S)\) can only be known by obtaining
experimental data. In these cases, canonicalization is skipped for the
entire task (since transforming the sequence would break the label)

Regions without transcribed features remain unmodified. Even if the
sequence falls on the negative strand and the model never saw it, I will
not take its RC in order to test the model's generalization.

It should also be noted that not all transcribed features are
canonicalized, since when there are overlaps, the canonicalization
algorithm has to make compromises in certain places. Even so, it is
assumed that all features were canonicalized correctly, since the goal
is to evaluate how well the model learns to capture transcriptional
asymmetries, not the compromises the canonicalization algorithm had to
make.

\subsection{Evaluation of the canonicalization algorithm}
\label{sec:evaluation_canonicalizaiton_algorithms}

To evaluate the canonicalization method (described more extensively below) the training data is
pre-processed, and the same pretraining and fine-tuning protocol as
in the ConvNova paper is used (except for the cases where it is
explicitly stated that a different protocol was used)

For each of the tasks, as with HyenaDNA, the model is pretrained on hg38
using MLM. 10\% of the nucleotides are masked and the model's objective
is to predict the masked nucleotides.

For the downstream tasks, simply comparing the performance of the non-canonicalized model vs the canonicalized one isn't enough, because the canonicalization algorithm uses additional data, introducing a data leakage that could be inflating the true improvement caused by the canonicalization algorithm.

In the gene finding task from the BEND Benchmark, for example, a task that
consists of classifying each nucleotide as exon, intron, acceptor,
donor, or nothing, the (noisy) introduction of the strand orientation data by the canonicalization algorithm produces a leakage.

To mitigate that, an ablation was designed where 3 different versions of the model are evaluated:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Baseline: The baseline model without any canonicalization
	\item Canonicalized: The model evaluated on the canonicalized datasets
	\item: Leakage control: The model evaluated on the non-canonicalized datasets with an extra channel on it's input indicating whether that nucleotide is a positively transcribed feature (+1), a negatively transcribed feature( -1) or isn't transcribed (0)
\end{itemize}

The evaluation protocol for a model consists of 5 runs with 5 different random seeds. Those 3 versions of the model are evaluated using that protocol on all downstream tasks using a fewer parameter configuration to speed up the evaluation(see section \ref{hyperparameter-search}). Then, for the tasks where a promising difference appears between the canonicalization model vs the baseline and leakage control models, that same evaluation protocol is used but with the original, larger configuration used in ConvNova.

The 5 random seeds used were: 1938, 205, 8262, 1480 and 4436.

\subsection{Downstream tasks}
For the choice of baselines and evaluation protocol we
follow the protocol established by the authors of ConvNova
\citep{boRevisitingConvolutionArchitecture2025}, unless stated
otherwise. The reason this protocol was used is that, by using ConvNova
as the architecture and only changing the data pre-processing, the
results are directly comparable.

All models were fine-tuned after being pretrained on hg38. For details
regarding how the labels of each downstream task were handled, see
section \ref{sec:downstream-task-canon-classification} The downstream tasks and their details are listed below. Each downstream task has a specific model configuration, see section \ref{sec:appendix_downstream_tasks} for more details.

\subsubsection{NT Benchmark} 
This benchmark is the one used by Nucleotide Transformer
(\cite{dalla-torreNucleotideTransformerBuilding2025}). It consists of 3
types of tasks: histone marker prediction, splice site annotation
prediction, and regulatory annotation prediction. It should be noted
that the most recent version of the benchmark was used, which was not
used in the original ConvNova paper.

To evaluate the tasks, the same methodology as in HyenaDNA
(\citep{nguyenHyenaDNALongRangeGenomic2023}) was followed, using the
Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) for certain tasks and the
F1-score for others. The results of the model with canonicalization are
compared against the model without canonicalization, and the rest of the
models against those the original ConvNova paper compared against.

For canonicalization, GRCh38 was used as the source genome and the
annotations were obtained from the Genome Reference Consortium, since all the datasets come from human samples. The tasks were evaluated using a pooling downstream head.

\subsubsection{Genomic Benchmark}
It is a multi-species benchmark, consisting of human, mouse,
roundworm, and fruit fly samples. Its tasks consist of enhancers,
species classification, and regulatory elements.

For canonicalization, the following source genomes were used: hg38 for
the human sample tasks, GRCm39 from the Genome Reference Consortium, and for the roundworm and drosophila sample
tasks the NCBI reference genomes were used. The tasks were evaluated using a pooling downstream head.

Keep in mind that for \texttt{human\_ensembl\_regulatory}, the model performance dropped compared to the original ConvNova paper because a longer sequence length was used, where the whole dataset sequence is passed to the model instead of a trimmed version of it like in the original ConvNova paper (see more details in section \ref{sec:appendix_downstream_tasks}). It's theorized that happens because of the pooling decoder because the signal is at the start of the sequence and the pooling decoder dilutes it with a longer sequence length.

\subsubsection{BEND Benchmark}
The model was evaluated only on the gene finding tasks, where given a DNA sequence
each nucleotide must be classified as intron, exon, or neither, and cpg
methylation, where given a DNA sequence multilabel classification is
performed on whether it is methylated or not in different cell lines.

The chromatin accessibility and histone modification tasks are redundant
compared to the tasks in the rest of the benchmarks, and therefore were
not included in the evaluation.

The zero-shot variant effects tasks were also omitted, since unlike
models with an attention mechanism, each output token was determined by
a very small local context, and if one wanted to mitigate that through
pooling, the SNP token signal becomes too diluted for classification.
Evaluating those tasks in that way gives artificially low results.

For CpG methylation a linear head was used over the entire encoded
sequence, and for gene finding each token is passed through a linear
layer.

\subsubsection{Chromatin Profile Prediction}
This task \citep{zhouPredictingEffectsNoncoding2015} consists of predicting
chromatin profiles and epigenetic markers given a DNA sequence. The
dataset consists of 919 chromatin features, grouped into transcription
factor binding profiles, DNase I hypersensitivity, and histone marks.

The source genome of DeepSEA is hg19. In this specific case, for
canonicalization, hg38 is used as the source genome in order to use the
most up-to-date annotations, and the coordinates are resolved directly
by aligning the DeepSEA sequences with minimap2
\citep{liMinimap2PairwiseAlignment2018} instead of using the GENCODE
lift-over.

\subsection{Optimal canonicalization parameters search}
\label{sec:methods_optimal_canonicalization_search}

The canonicalization pipeline and its parameters were optimized using Optuna \citep{akibaOptunaNextgenerationHyperparameter2019} using its default TPE optimizer. 20 trials were run to optimize the following parameters:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \texttt{annotations\_level}: Filter the GENCODE annotations to use
	annotations up to level 1, 2, or 3.
	\item \texttt{transformation}: Whether to transform a segment (and the
	overlaps window) by taking the reverse-complement, the complement, or
	inverting it.
	\item \texttt{overlapped\_segments\_break\_entirely}: If set to True, it is
	then assumed that when transforming a feature A that overlaps with a
	feature B that's in the opposite orientation, both A and B are broken, making
	it most convenient to ignore any segment that overlaps.
	\item \texttt{partial\_canonicalization\_works}: When canonicalizing a segment
	that overlaps with another, whether to consider that the entire
	partially canonicalized segment breaks, or only the part that was
	overlapping breaks. The optimal value was to consider that the entire
	segment breaks
	\item \texttt{include\_cvdj\_segments}: Whether to count the
	cvdj segments of the human and mouse adaptive immune system as
	transcribed features or not.
	\item \texttt{include\_pseudogenes}: Whether to count pseudogenes or not as
	transcribed features.
\end{itemize}

See section \ref{sec:design-of-the-canonicalization-algorithm} for more details on ===(TODO: THE VARIABLES USED)=== and a more detailed explanation on the different canonicalization algorithms and the way they deal with overlapping features.

Only 20 trials were used because even though the amount of possible configurations (96) is much larger than 32, most of the hyperparameters had little to no importance. See section \ref{sec:hyperparameter-optimization-results} for more details on the results.

To speed up the trials, the model was pretrained and fine-tuned with a
single configuration, in order to obtain representative metrics for the
configuration without having to wait too long. See section
\ref{hyperparameter-search} for more details on the model configuration.

For more details about the hyperparameter optimization results, see section \ref{sec:hyperparameter-optimization-results}

Each trial was evaluated on all the downstream tasks, and their score was computed by computing and averaging the z-score for all downstream tasks. The mean and variance for those z-scores were obtained from the non-canonicalized baseline previously mentioned on section \ref{sec:evaluation_canonicalizaiton_algorithms}. 

Because there is a considerable task type imbalance (there are many more histone marks related tasks than splicing related tasks for example), the tasks scores were split into groups, and then the average of each group was averaged together. See section \ref{sec:hpo_task_grouping} for more details on how the tasks were grouped together.

\subsection{Future work}
Regarding future work, one could:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Make the
	canonicalization pipeline work with an algorithm that does not require
	external annotations, such as a neural network for example, so that the
	model can achieve superior performance without needing to incorporate
	additional data, and so that it works only with the DNA sequence without
	needing extra data.
	
	In order to train such a network end-to-end, since the \(\arg\max\) that
	decides whether to canonicalize the DNA strand is not differentiable, a
	straight-through estimator could be used to mitigate this
	\citep{bengioEstimatingPropagatingGradients2013}

	\item Evaluate this canonicalization proof
	of concept on other, non-convolutional models to assess its
	generalization capacity. For example, it could be incorporated into
	HyenaDNA and evaluated against Caduceus, its RC-equivariant variant.
	\item Evaluate a proof of concept incorporating the recognition of origin
	recognition complexes (ORC) into the canonicalization pipeline, in order
	to evaluate the performance of a model that works with the replicational
	asymmetry between the leading and lagging strands.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Design of the canonicalization algorithm for overlapping transcripts}\label{sec:design-of-the-canonicalization-algorithm}
Regarding the approach to dealing with overlapping transcribed
features, 3 variables were proposed with which to formulate all the
hypotheses of the optimal algorithm: 

\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{\texttt{overlap\_transformation\_behavior}}: Determines the different hypotheses for what happens when canonicalizing a segment \textbf{A} that overlaps with a segment \textbf{B}:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \emph{Partial break}: Only the overlapping part between \textbf{A} and \textbf{B} of both features breaks.
		\item \emph{Full break}: The entire segment \textbf{B} is broken when partially
		canonicalizing one of its parts.
		\item \emph{Both break}: Both \textbf{A} and \textbf{B} are entirely broken, since that overlapping part needed to be kept intact for both features to be understood correctly by the model.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{overlap_break_behavior.png}
	\end{figure}
	
	\item \emph{\texttt{orient\_all\_segments}}: A binary variable indicating whether it
	is necessary for all features to be in the canonical orientation, or
	whether to not canonicalize certain features if the cost of ignoring
	them (the number of nucleotides) is lower than the cost of
	canonicalizing them (the number of nucleotides it destroys, which are
	calculated depending on \texttt{overlap\_transformation\_behavior}). The
	only case where it is not irrelevant is where
	\texttt{overlap\_transformation\_behavior = full\_break}; it is irrelevant in
	the rest of the cases. This is explained in more detail below.
	
	\item \emph{\texttt{keep\_segments\_order}}: In the case where
	\texttt{overlap\_transformation\_behavior = full\_break} and
	\texttt{orient\_all\_segments=True} there are 2 ways to canonicalize the
	features, one that breaks the fewest nucleotides but destroys the
	original order of the features, and another where that original order is
	kept but more nucleotides are destroyed. This variable decides which of
	the 2 ways to use. They are described in more detail below
\end{itemize}

Based on all the possible permutations of these variables, the following
canonicalization methods arise:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item If \texttt{overlap\_transformation\_behavior=partial\_break},
	\texttt{orient\_all\_segments} (and \texttt{keep\_segments\_order}) have no effect since the cost of ignoring a segment is always \(\geq\) the cost
	of canonicalizing it, since the length of the overlap is always less
	than or equal to the length of the segment, so all features are
	oriented, regardless of whether there is an overlap or not
	\\
	\item If \texttt{overlap\_transformation\_behavior=both\_break}, the rest of the
	variables have no effect, since the cost of ignoring \textbf{B}
	(length(\textbf{B})) is always less than the cost of canonicalizing it
	(length(\textbf{A}) + length( \textbf{B} )), so only the features that have no overlap at
	all are oriented
	\\
	\item If \texttt{overlap\_transformation\_behavor=full\_break}, there are 3
	possible canonicalization algorithms. They all rely on building a \emph{conflict window} first. When a negative segment that overlaps with a positive one is found, the entire conflict window is computed recursively, the set of the whole ``chain'' of overlapping features.
	
	The reason conflict windows are used instead of simply looking at
	immediate overlaps, is that there are cases where only looking at
	immediate overlaps ends up in a bad decision. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:conflict_window_example}, for example,
	if I decide whether to canonicalize \textbf{A} or not:
	\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*]
		\item If I only check the immediate overlaps, then I do not canonicalize \textbf{A} since it is
		preferable to destroy it rather than \textbf{B}, (because \textbf{B} is longer)
		
		\item If I check the entire conflict window, it is preferable for me to canonicalize the
		negative features (100bp) and destroy \textbf{B} rather than ignore them.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{conflict_window.png}
		\caption{Example showing the reason behind the usage of conflict windows}
		\label{fig:conflict_window_example}
	\end{figure}
	
	After the conflict window is built, the canonicalization algorithm branches in:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item If \texttt{orient\_all\_segments=False}, then the cheapest option is chosen. Orient the negative features at the cost of destroying the positive features (the sum of the lengths of positive features in the conflict window), or ignore the negative features, at a cost equal to the sum of the lengths of the negative features.
		
		\item If \texttt{orient\_all\_segments=True}, then there are 2 ways to deal with the overlapping features:
		
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item If one wishes to keep the exact order of the features as they were (\texttt{keep\_segments\_order=True}), then, across the whole genome, one can either canonicalize the features from the - to the + strand in all of the conflict windows, or canonicalize the features from the + to the - strand, and then take the RC of the entire genome to bring them all to the + strand.
			
			\medskip
			Although the original order of the features is not maintained when applying the second option, relative order of ``which one follows which'' is still maintained. An example of this canonicalization method can be seen on Figure~\ref{fig:canon_ordered_segments}
			
			\begin{figure}[H]
				\centering
				\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ordered_segments_canonicalization.png}
				\caption{}
				\captionsetup{labelsep=none}
				\label{fig:canon_ordered_segments}
			\end{figure}
			
			
			\item If the canonicalization with the lowest total cost is desired, regardless of the order of the features, a procedure similar to the previous one is applied but with scope at the overlap window level instead of the genomic level. The reason the order is lost is that some overlap windows are reversed and others are not, causing the order to be lost at the genomic level. An example of this method can be seen below on Figure~\ref{fig:keep_order_false_example}
			
			\begin{figure}[H]
				\centering
				\includegraphics[height=0.4\textheight]{keep_order_false_example.png}
				\caption{}
				\captionsetup{labelsep=none}
				\label{fig:keep_order_false_example}
			\end{figure}
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

The advantages and disadvantages of the possible configurations of
\texttt{keep\_segments\_order} can be seen on Figure~\ref{fig:full_break_examples}.
\begin{itemize}
	\item On the case where \texttt{keep\_segments\_order=True}, the canonicalization is done that way because destroying the B, D and F features (110bp) is cheaper than destroying the A, C and E features(210bp)
	\item On the case where \texttt{keep\_segments\_order=False} each overlap window is processed individually, and even though the result destroys the least amount of nucleotides, the order is lost(as seen in the second conflicts window)
\end{itemize}

	\caption{}
	\captionsetup{labelsep=none}
	\label{fig:full_break_examples}
\end{figure}
